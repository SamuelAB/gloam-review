Gloam VR: approach to the rain

The technology we use, the method we hold it to, what we have retired along the way and why, and what comes next. Written in October 2026; dates below are from the project's commit history.

What we are building, and why

Gloam VR is a walkable virtual-reality sculpture: a pierced limestone dome that the visitor explores on foot, crouching and sitting inside it. We are adding weather to it, starting with rain that behaves like real water on real stone. The visitor is always inside the sculpture, so the work is judged from inside: water running down the inner walls, coming in through the openings, and collecting on the floor.

The aim is rain whose behaviour comes from physics rather than from effects tuned by eye. Drops fall at the speed their size dictates; the porous stone drinks water, darkens, and only sheds water once it is full; puddles hold edges set by surface tension; splashes and ripples follow published measurements. A stain on the stone is a record of where water actually went.

No existing tool does this at the scale of the sculpture. Its wet surface is about 190 square metres, and a raindrop is 0.5 to 4 mm across. A full 3D fluid simulation fine enough to resolve drops and films over that area would need on the order of 1011 cells. So we use reduced models (drops as particles, thin water layers on the surfaces, absorption into the stone) and keep the full 3D solvers as references to check them against.

Technology

Our own GPU rain solver, written with NVIDIA Warp
Warp is a Python framework for writing GPU code. On it we built the parts no off-the-shelf engine provides:
  • Falling rain: drop sizes from the Marshall-Palmer distribution, fall speeds from Gunn and Kinzer's measurements, and the flattened (not teardrop) shape of real falling drops.
  • Floor water: a finite-volume shallow-water solver on 4 mm cells (a standard scheme from hydraulics: MUSCL reconstruction, Audusse hydrostatic reconstruction for wet and dry ground, HLL fluxes, friction, Heun time stepping). On top of it, contact-line physics: a puddle edge only advances when the water stands high enough to overcome surface tension (about 2 to 3.5 mm, set by the contact angle), edges are smoothed by curvature, they move at speeds from the Cox-Voinov law, and thin films on non-absorbent floors break up into holes and beads.
  • Stone: absorption by sorptivity (the square-root-of-time law for porous materials, with a published limestone value) and sideways wicking through the pores.
  • Splash and ripples: secondary droplets whose counts, sizes, speeds and angles come from published drop-impact studies; rings on standing water from a damped wave model.
  • Falling water: a module that turns a source (a thrown bucket, or a falling stream) into water that flies with air drag and lands with its momentum.
  • Catchment map: 650,000 particles dropped over the whole site to find which rain actually ends up inside, so detail is spent where a visitor can see it.
Basilisk, an independent reference
An open-source research fluid solver (two-phase, with surface tension and contact angle). We run the same case in Basilisk and in our solver and compare them side by side.
Blender for review renders
Clay renders (grey stone, blue water) in EEVEE, so shape and behaviour can be judged without lighting getting in the way. No motion blur and no depth of field: drops are drawn at their true size, as the headset shows them.
Unity for the headset
The sculpture runs in Unity at 90 frames per second on an RTX 3090. The plan for rain there is to run the fast layers (wetness, films, ripples) as GPU compute on the visible surfaces, draw falling rain and splashes as GPU particles, and bake only slow fields such as the catchment map.

Method

This is a research project, not trial and error. Every change follows the same steps, recorded in a living research plan:

  1. Research first. Find the governing physics and its constants in the literature, with sources and the range in which they hold.
  2. Compare approaches. At least two candidate algorithms, including how established codes solve the same problem, and a written reason for the choice.
  3. Physical parameters only. Any number without a source is listed as a model choice, a debt to be justified by a measurement or replaced.
  4. Validate up a ladder before anything is rendered for review:
    • L0: automated tests against exact solutions (absorption over time, puddle edge height, the dam-break solution, terminal speeds, wave damping);
    • L1: published experiments (how far drops spread, splash counts and angles);
    • L2: the same case in a reference solver (Basilisk);
    • L3: photographs and video of the real thing, including published footage;
    • L4: the artist's review, in the headset or on this site.
  5. Record what failed as well as what worked.

A picture that looks wrong is never fixed by turning a knob: the question is which physics is missing or which constant is wrong. On top of the tests, the code is audited adversarially: independent AI reviewers are asked to find what is broken, each finding is checked, and a fix only counts once a test exists that would have caught the bug. Audits ran on October 9, 10 and 11, 2026.

Retired, and why

Blender's FLIP fluid for whole-sculpture rainturned off Oct 9
A general 3D fluid solver cannot reach millimetre scale over the whole sculpture, and the runs were too heavy. Full 3D solvers are now used only as references on small cases.
Traces drawn as ribbons over the stoneOct 9
Ribbons floated above the surface or sank into it. Water traces are now painted into the stone itself, as stains. See the progress archive.
Motion blur in review rendersOct 9
A headset shows each instant sharp, so the renders now do too, with drops at their true size.
"Thin film means dry ground" and a hand-set edge tensionOct 10
A floor cell whose water fell below a threshold was treated as dry, which froze spreading water and left a falsely dry middle with specks. Research showed such a film is still wet until a hole actually opens; cells now remember being wet. A free edge-tension setting was replaced by the curvature term from capillary physics, which has no knob.
The virtual-pipe floor solverOct 10
The first floor model moved water between cells through "virtual pipes", a common graphics technique. A comparison with Basilisk exposed one error in it, and then the exact dam-break test (Ritter's solution) showed its front running at about 40 % of the correct speed: it has no momentum advection, fine for slow films but wrong for spills, runoff and water landing hard. It was replaced by a finite-volume shallow-water solver, the approach used by established codes, which matches Ritter's front (0.354 m against 0.352 m exact).
Forward-Euler time steppingOct 10
A disturbed puddle on a flat floor gained energy instead of losing it: the time stepping itself was adding energy. Replaced by Heun's method (a two-stage scheme used by the reference codes). The leftover unevenness of a settled basin dropped from 3.56 mm to 0.13 mm, and a new test checks that waves always lose energy.
A level-set contact linebuilt and set aside Oct 10
To make spreading puddles rounder on the square grid, we built a sub-cell front (the remedy used in solidification and wildfire modelling). On its own it was the roundest, but the geometric front and the actual water came apart, leaving a ragged skirt of empty cells ahead of the puddle. It is kept, switched off, and a narrower grid rule was adopted. A small four-fold imprint (2 to 3 %, target 1.5 %) remains, recorded as open.
The 10-second "bucket"Oct 11
The spill test released a bucket over 10 seconds at a fixed impact speed; a real throw is over in well under a second. Fixing it also revealed that thrown water lost all its forward momentum on dry floor, so every "throw" so far had landed as if dropped. A physical throw model (release, flight with drag, landing with momentum) replaced it.
The first wall model, set aside for now
Beads, films, trails and stains on the sculpture walls were built before this method was adopted (see the archive). Work moved to the floor first; the walls come back under the same method later.

Trying next

  1. Close the October 11 audit. Its open findings include momentum lost in partly filled cells, very light rain on deep water vanishing to rounding error, walls wrongly counted as dry floor at puddle edges, and GPU results that differ slightly between identical runs. Deterministic sums come first, so the test suite is reproducible.
  2. Finish floor spreading, then compare against published experiments: a water jet on a dry plate filmed from first contact (Mallik et al., 2026), and the classic collapse of a water column.
  3. Slopes and rivulets: the same solver on an incline, checked against the measured finger spacing of water running down a slope.
  4. Splash reference: single drops in Basilisk, and a published crown-splash benchmark, against our splash model.
  5. Walls and drips: bring the sculpture walls up to this method (a thin-film model on the mesh; drip size from Tate's law).
  6. Drying and appearance, then back to the sculpture and into the headset.

Measurements we would like: a soak test on a tile of the sculpture's stone (its absorption rate is currently a published limestone value, not measured), and filmed spills of known volume with a ruler in frame.

An idea under consideration, not started: small, softly glowing openings inside the cave that pour water, from a trickle to a fall, after Japanese waterfall shrines. The falling-water module was built so it can be reused for them.

Known limits today

  • No evaporation yet: in the current floor bench, damp stone never dries.
  • The stone has no colour texture in review renders; they show shape and behaviour, not final look.
  • Puddles on a perfectly uniform floor still carry the small four-fold imprint described above.
