Gloam VR: approach

How the work is being made, the method it is held to, what was tried and set aside along the way, and what comes next. Written in plain language, October 2026.

What we are building, and why

Gloam is a vast cave, hand-sculpted in virtual reality over four years, that the visitor walks through in a headset with nothing to hold and nothing to press. A complete cycle of natural light moves through it. We are now turning it into a living place: weather (rain that behaves like real water on real stone, wind, dust), a ground under the sculpture shaped by years of that weather, and a custom player built for it. The weather is studied today as offline renders, made frame by frame.

The visitor is always inside, so the work is judged from inside: water running down the inner walls, coming in through the openings and gathering on the floor; dust settling in the light; the ground carrying the history of the place.

The aim is behaviour that comes from nature rather than from effects adjusted by eye. Drops fall at the speed their size gives them; porous stone drinks water and darkens before it sheds any; puddles hold their edges the way surface tension holds real ones; splashes follow measurements of real drops. A stain on the stone is a record of where water actually went.

What we use

Our own water simulation, running on the graphics card
No existing tool can simulate rain at the scale of the sculpture (about 380 square metres of stone, drops a few millimetres across), so we write our own, focused on what matters at this scale: falling drops, splashes, water films and beads on the stone, absorption into its pores, drying, and the water that gathers on the floor.
Independent reference simulations
A research-grade fluid simulator, run on small cases, to check our own results against.
Gloam's own real-time player
The sculpture runs today in Unity. Gloam now also has its own real-time player, built for it, which removes the stutter Unity has when it loads each new step of the light and can trace light directly against the full sculpture on the graphics card. For the weather studies it makes offline renders, frame by frame, in which a puddle reflects the actual cave.
Study renders
Simple grey-stone, blue-water renders, so behaviour can be judged without lighting getting in the way. No motion blur and no depth of field: drops are shown at their true size, as a VR display shows them.

Method

This is a research project, not trial and error. Every change follows the same steps:

  1. Research first: find what the scientific literature says about the phenomenon, with sources.
  2. Compare approaches, including how established tools handle the same problem, and write down the reason for the choice.
  3. Physical values only: any number without a source is listed as a debt, to be measured or replaced.
  4. Check before showing: automatic tests against exact answers, then published experiments, then the reference simulations, then photographs and film of the real thing, and finally the artist's review.
  5. Record what failed as well as what worked (below).

A picture that looks wrong is never fixed by turning a knob: the question is which piece of nature is missing. Independent AI reviewers are also asked to find what is broken; each finding is checked, and a fix only counts once a test exists that would have caught the problem.

Set aside, and why

A general-purpose fluid simulator for the whole sculptureOct 9
It could not reach millimetre detail over the whole cave, and runs were far too heavy. Such tools are now used only as references on small cases.
Water traces drawn as ribbons over the stoneOct 9
They floated above the surface or sank into it. Traces are now painted into the stone itself, as stains.
Motion blur in review rendersOct 9
A headset shows each instant sharp, so the renders now do too.
The first floor-water modelOct 10
A common graphics technique, but checked against an exact answer it moved spreading water at about 40 % of the right speed. Replaced by a standard approach from hydraulics engineering, which matches the exact answer.
Edges that ran ahead of their waterOct 11
A new test checks that a spreading puddle's edge moves only as fast as water can reach it. It was moving 1.5 times too fast; corrected, and puddles came out rounder too.
Experiments that did not make itOct 10 and 11
Several ideas were built, measured and set aside because they made other things worse: a sharper way of drawing puddle edges, an extra model of ripple damping that turned out to count damping twice, and a rule meant to make edges spread equally in every direction that broke four other checks. One wrong diagnosis is recorded too: we believed thrown water stayed blotchy; measured, the water was flat, and the blotches were the floor's own small bumps showing through a depth-coloured view.
The first wall model, set aside for now
Beads, films, trails and stains on the walls were built before this method was adopted (see the archive). They will come back under the same method.

Next

  1. The floor and the sculpture in the same storm: the rain on the stone and the water on the floor, from one simulation, seen together.
  2. The sound of the rain: each drop's sound from its size, speed and what it lands on, carried through the cave.
  3. Dust and water together: the space starts with its own patina; rain cleans dust realistically, partly and never completely; drying puddles leave faint rings; wind brings dust back in.

Measurements we would like: a soak test on a tile of real stone, and filmed spills of known size with a ruler in frame.

Known limits today

  • Straight puddle edges spread about 9 % slower in some directions than others; round puddles are round to 1 %.
  • How thin water breaks into small puddles on a non-absorbing floor is still a placeholder.
  • Study renders show shape and behaviour, not final colour or light.
